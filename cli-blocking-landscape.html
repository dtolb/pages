Where a local caller ID stops working.

A growing set of countries block inbound international calls that present one of their own national numbers as the caller ID. Nine European markets do it today — almost all of them switched on during 2024 — plus three attested outside Europe. The rules do not attach to the number; they attach to the anonymous international gateway. That distinction is the whole game: traffic delivered over an identified in-country route is outside the blocking duty by design, and at least three regulators say so in writing. This page is the lay of the land plus the specific route out of each market.

01

The three things that matter

The mechanism

It's the gateway, not the number

Every instrument examined triggers on an interface where the operator cannot identify the caller. Finland defines this explicitly, then says foreign-located call centres may use Finnish CLI over a dedicated route. Same carve-out in Sweden, Belgium, and the pan-European recommendation.

The asymmetry

Geographic is flat, mobile is conditional

Geographic and toll-free CLI get blocked outright. Mobile CLI is usually blocked only after the operator verifies the subscriber isn't roaming. Ireland and Belgium check against live roaming data. This asymmetry propagates from one CEPT recommendation into nearly every national rule.

The trap

Three different remedies, one word

"Blocked" is used loosely for three outcomes with three different symptoms: the call fails, the call completes with the caller ID stripped (Germany, Austria), or the call completes at a penalty rate. Only the first shows up as an ASR drop.

03

What to do about it, market by market

One architecture covers most of the list. The rest are exceptions worth knowing because they fail in unintuitive ways — a number type that's banned regardless of origin, a rule that punishes an empty CLI, a market where the constraint isn't a CLI rule at all.

Play 01 · The main one

Dedicated in-country interconnect

Applies to: Finland · Sweden · Ireland · Belgium · Malta · and presumptively Poland, Spain, UK

Sanctioned in writing

These rules do not attach to the number. They attach to an interface defined by the operator's inability to identify the caller. Deliver the same traffic over a route where the subscriber is identifiable and their right to use the number is verifiable, and the blocking duty does not apply — not as a loophole, but by the definition in the regulation.

"public international interface means an interface through which a telecommunications operator receives terminating interconnection traffic from different originating networks without being able to identify the calling party subscription or the originating network" Traficom Regulation 28 L/2025, §2(10) — the trigger
"when traffic is directed to Finland so that the subscriber using a Finnish telephone number can be identified and the subscriber's right to use the telephone number can be verified in accordance with sections 10.1 and 10.2" §7.2.2 — the exception
"In this context, it is irrelevant whether the customer is physically located in Finland or abroad… Thus, for example, corporate switchboards and call centres may be physically located abroad and still use Finnish telephone numbers as long as the traffic is directed to Finland via a dedicated traffic route so that a customer or telecommunications operator using Finnish numbers can be identified and the telecommunications operator of the originating network can comply with Regulation 28." Traficom explanatory memorandum — the regulator describing our exact use case

The same carve-out, in the same shape, elsewhere:

  • Sweden — PTSFS 2024:2 §6 exempts cases "där trafiken kan föras till Sverige på ett sätt som gör att samtalet inte kommer in till Sverige via ett internationellt samtrafikgränssnitt."
  • Belgium — KB art. 4 names nomadic, cloud conferencing, customer-support and direct-marketing calls delivered "via een speciale interface" under the originating operator's full control.
  • CEPT — ECC/REC/(23)03 names exactly two sanctioned remedies for cloud providers: a per-number whitelist, or a dedicated in-country SIP/trunk interface. It notes the second brings the provider under the national regulator (EECC Art. 97(2)).

The cost: in-country regulatory exposure, and per-market build. That is the trade — and it is the one the regulators intend you to make.

Play 02

Per-number CLI whitelist or registration

Applies to: anywhere the terminating carrier or regulator operates one — confirm per market

Lower lift, patchy availability

ECC/REC/(23)03's first named remedy: register the specific national numbers permitted as CLI over cloud-based origination. Cheaper than an interconnect and doesn't change routing, but availability is inconsistent and per-number lead times are real. Worth asking carrier relations before assuming an interconnect build is the only path.

Play 03

Mobile CLI where geographic is blocked

Applies to: Czechia — and essentially nowhere else

One market only

Czechia's ČTÚ mandate covers non-mobile +420 CLI only; mobile was deliberately left to self-regulation. That makes a Czech mobile presentation number a genuine option where a geographic one fails.

Do not generalise this. Everywhere else, mobile CLI is blocked conditionally — permitted only after the terminating operator verifies the subscriber is actually roaming. Ireland checks against its Roamer Check facility, Belgium requires verification under art. 1,3°, Saudi Arabia and Oman use SS7 roaming queries. Genuine roaming is not a business architecture, so this is not a workaround in those markets.

Play 04 · Special case

Norway: two rules, one of them absolute

Applies to: Norway

No workaround for offshore operations

Norway's blocking rule is mobile-scoped and textually unusual — ekomforskriften §9-5 keys on absence of a right to use the A-number rather than on the international interface. Carriers (Telenor, Telia, Ice, with NRDB) run a coordinated inbound "grensekontroll" on mobile traffic from abroad.

But two separate Nkom numbering rules bite regardless of any CLI regime, and these are the ones that actually kill the use case:

  • Call centres may not use 8-digit Norwegian mobile numbers as outbound presentation numbers at all. Fixed or special numbers only.
  • "It is not permitted for e.g., customer centres or call centres to use Norwegian numbers, if the business is located abroad."

The second is a flat prohibition on the offshore pattern, independent of how the traffic is delivered. An in-country interconnect does not cure it. Source: Nkom numbering resources.

Play 05

Germany and Austria: expect a stripped CLI, not a failure

Applies to: Germany · Austria

Calls complete — caller ID does not

Both mandate suppression rather than blocking. The call connects; the recipient sees no number. This will not appear as an ASR drop, so it's easy to miss in monitoring while quietly destroying answer rates and callback ability.

Germany, TKG 2021 §120(4): "…die Rufnummernanzeige ist zu unterdrücken. Ausgenommen von Satz 1 ist das internationale Roaming im Mobilfunk." Austria's KEM-V §5a makes suppression mandatory and blocking permissive — so an Austrian carrier may additionally choose to drop the call.

One live trap: i3Forum warns that suppression upstream can trigger blocking downstream — "such calls may fail if the regulations in the receiving country B require that incoming international calls must be blocked with an empty CLI field." Verify per market before defaulting to a withheld CLI.

Play 06 · Always available

Present a foreign CLI

Applies to: every market on this page

Always survives

A non-national presentation number is what these rules are built to permit. It survives everywhere and needs no registration or interconnect. The cost is the entire reason customers wanted a local number: local-presence answer rates.

For Saudi Arabia, Oman and China this is the safe default today. Note their attested mechanism is a mobile roaming check, so geographic CLI may still be viable — but that is unverified and worth a carrier question rather than an assumption.

Play 07

Surcharge markets: check margin, not delivery

Applies to: Greece, Denmark, Cyprus, Bulgaria, Hungary, Slovakia, Lithuania (low confidence)

Silent cost, not a failure

A single vendor source reports a penalty surcharge rather than blocking from 1 July 2025. No primary instrument was located, so treat the country list as unconfirmed — but the failure mode is worth acting on regardless, because a surcharge shows up as eroded margin on successful calls and will never appear in delivery metrics. Cheap to check against rate decks.

Play 08

Colombia: use it now, watch for the trigger

Applies to: Colombia

Unrestricted today

CRC Resolución 8308 (30 July 2026) adds the blocking mandate but it is not yet exigible. Commencement is not a calendar date — it triggers three months after a later CRC resolution adopting the CTLFSM committee's recommendation on international roaming traffic, which has not issued.

When it does bite, scope is unusually broad: the entire Plan Nacional de Numeración with no carve-out by number type, so geographic, mobile and 01800 toll-free all fall in together. The same numeral binds international carriers to E.164 formats and to respect the true origin country code, foreclosing A-number refile.

Action: Colombian local CLI is fine today. Put a watch on CRC publications for the triggering resolution, and treat the three-month clock as your migration window.

Play 09 · Reframe

UAE: the constraint probably isn't CLI

Applies to: United Arab Emirates

Ask a different question

No TDRA CLI instrument exists publicly. The regulations index has categories for Numbering, Interconnection, VOIP and Spam with no documents under them. The 2007 inbound-international-traffic decision never mentions caller ID. The only caller-ID item in TDRA's entire sitemap is a 2022 press release about Kashif — CNAP name display — and e&'s own page says it "only works while in the UAE." i3Forum's per-country table has no UAE row.

What the vendor evidence actually shows is a number availability and licensing constraint: UAE local, mobile, national and shared-cost numbers listed as unavailable by some providers; toll-free gated on a Letter of Intent; two-way service gated on a UAE Trade License. Meanwhile several providers do sell two-way service presenting a +971 CLI. What differs between them is where the call originates and whether the customer holds a UAE licence — not whether the gateway rewrites CLI.

Action: resolve this by asking whether a licensed in-country entity exists, and by putting the question to e&/du wholesale directly. Do not go looking for a CLI regulation.

Play 10 · Non-issue

Egypt: nothing to work around

Applies to: Egypt

No restriction found

Egypt appears on informal blocker lists but the evidence does not support it. NTRA's 2024–25 programme regulates Egyptian mobile lines used for domestic telemarketing: register your numbers with the Egyptian operators, consumers then see the calling entity's name or an "NTRA Alert" label. The mechanism is CNAM. Enforcement has been against Egypt-established real-estate developers. May 2025 extended it to landline and added IMEI-level handset barring inside Egypt.

Across fourteen documents including all five official NTRA releases, there are zero mentions of inbound international calls, the international gateway, caller-ID spoofing, calls from abroad presenting a +20 number, or any operator duty to filter international traffic. The only "blocking" is of Egyptian subscribers and handsets.

Two caveats. Late-2025 Egyptian "unknown numbers / caller-name" initiatives were not fetched — if inbound-international CLI is addressed anywhere, that later track is where to look. And regional phrasing in industry commentary ("Europe, India and the Middle East") names no Middle East country; the only named MENA entries anywhere are Saudi Arabia and Oman. Do not read regional language as covering Egypt.

Play 11

Unverified markets: verify before advising

Applies to: France · Italy · Singapore · Malaysia · Taiwan · Australia · Denmark · Netherlands

No position yet

These are on the page so they aren't silently omitted, not because there's a recommendation attached. France's CPCE art. L. 44 IV mandates interruption but on authentication failure rather than origin-plus-national-CLI, and France was not ticked in the BEREC survey. Italy's AGCOM 106/25/CONS surfaced only via a webinar deck. The APAC entries rest on one vendor list. Denmark and the Netherlands were never located — which is not the same as cleared.

For any of these, the honest customer answer is "we don't know yet, and here's how we'd find out" — see the open items below.

04

Why these rules all look the same

The 2024 European wave isn't a coincidence. It traces to one CEPT recommendation, which is why the same asymmetry and the same carve-outs keep reappearing in different languages.

The source pattern

ECC/REC/(23)03 — approved 28 November 2023

"in general, it cannot be justified that calls originating from abroad can be associated with national fixed/geographic numbers." The rationale
"for national geographic/fixed E.164 numbers: to preferably block incoming international voice calls, or at least to suppress the CLI… except in justified cases."

"for national mobile E.164 numbers: to preferably block… or at least to suppress the CLI… after checking and verifying that the user is not roaming abroad, except in justified cases." The operative asymmetry — docdb.cept.org/download/4402

Geographic flat-blocked, mobile conditional on roaming state. That single distinction propagates into nearly every national instrument in the table above, and it is why mobile CLI behaves differently from geographic almost everywhere.

Scope limit worth remembering: CEPT has 46 European members. This instrument does not reach MENA, APAC or the Americas — so it explains the European cluster and tells you nothing about Egypt, the Gulf, or Latin America.

The wholesale industry's own norm cuts in our favour, and names our exposure directly. From i3Forum: "Transit and terminating operators must not alter CLI Data" — and "CLI removal policies can impact legitimate Enterprise DID and Call Center traffic due to the removal or blocking of domestic non-mobile CLI found in these international calls."

Questions for carrier relations

  • Per Tier 1 market: does the terminating carrier offer a CLI whitelist, and what is the per-number lead time?
  • Where we already have in-country interconnect, does our path actually satisfy the "identified interface" test — or are we transiting an anonymous gateway and being blocked without knowing?
  • Do we present an empty CLI anywhere into a market that blocks empty CLI?
  • Do we detect suppression (DE, AT) distinctly from blocking, and surface the difference?
  • Saudi Arabia and Oman block via mobile roaming checks — does that leave +966/+968 geographic CLI viable?
  • Are the 1 July 2025 surcharge markets hitting margin unnoticed?
  • UAE: CLI rule, or licensing prohibition plus number unavailability? Ask e&/du wholesale.

Document hygiene traps

  • Traficom overwrites the same PDF filename on each revision, so superseded versions vanish; its landing page also shows a stale "Valid from: 09/11/2023". Trust the PDF header block.
  • Finland's English text is headed "UNOFFICIAL TRANSLATION" on every page. Cite the Finnish for anything legal.
  • Finland's often-quoted phase-in dates (geographic 1 Jul 2022, mobile 2 Oct 2023) could not be confirmed against the superseded 28 J/2022 and are moot — the current 28 L/2025 has no transitional provisions for call blocking.
  • i3Forum "CLI Safe Zone" is Release 1.0, November 2023 with table status July/August 2023; its WordPress upload path misleadingly reads /2024/10/. Cite both dates.
The cheapest validation isn't more desk research. Blocking shows up in CDRs. An ASR comparison on national-CLI traffic into Finland, Sweden, Ireland, Belgium, Czechia, Malta, Poland, Spain and the UK would confirm or refute most of the table above in an afternoon — and would catch the markets this research never reached.
