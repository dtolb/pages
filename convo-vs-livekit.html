Internal · Competitive architecture dossier · flight-sandbox “Delta Assist” · 2026-07-20

Same demo, two architectures — and they aren’t the same product.

LiveKit sells media infrastructure with an agent framework on top. Twilio Agent Connect sells a managed conversation layer around your brain. A feature-by-feature bake-off misses the point: everything above the audio — threading, memory, post-call intelligence, and all of messaging — simply does not exist on the LiveKit side. It becomes the customer’s engineering backlog. This dossier maps Delta Assist onto both stacks, marks the honest wins in each direction, and ends with the plays for a LiveKit-shaped deal.

The honest ledger

What this repo’s own migration research concluded — written to plan a move to LiveKit, so it is not marketing in either direction.

What LiveKit genuinely gives them

Concede these early — credibility is the currency.
  • Full audio ownership: provider mixing, mid-call model changes, latency tuning, realtime speech-to-speech as an option, first-class recording.
  • Materially better turn detection and interruption handling than ConversationRelay’s built-ins.
  • Best-in-class dev observability: per-stage latency traces (LLM TTFT, TTS TTFB, end-of-utterance delay) with synchronized audio replay.
  • Browser voice for free — WebRTC is the native transport, telephony is the add-on.
  • Open-source framework (Apache 2.0) with real momentum: OpenAI’s Advanced Voice Mode runs on it; ~25% of US 911 dispatch centers; $1B valuation Jan ’26.
  • Warm/cold transfer + DTMF for contact-center patterns.

What they give up — and now own

Each bullet is a yellow “NEW” box in diagram B.
  • Threading: Orchestrator’s stable cross-channel conversation ID is gone — mint your own, join channels by phone, define session boundaries yourself.
  • Memory: Recall’s auto-extracted observations become a hand-built summary store whose quality is now a product surface.
  • Post-call classification: the CONVERSATION_END → operator → webhook chain becomes DIY hooks — and SMS needs an inactivity sweeper the platform used to provide silently.
  • An audio engineering competency: STT/TTS/VAD/turn-detection choices, a latency budget, and a new always-on runtime to operate and page on.
  • All of messaging: SMS, and with it the proactive “we’ll text you back” motion — unless they keep Twilio, which they will.
  • Single-vendor accountability: the voice path now spans Twilio trunk → LiveKit SIP → their worker → 2–3 model vendors.

Selling against LiveKit — five plays

Built from actually planning a migration onto their stack — these are the pressure points that survived contact with the details.

Play 1 · Reframe

“LiveKit replaces Twilio” is false — even in their docs

LiveKit’s telephony quickstart is literally “connect your Twilio Elastic SIP trunk.” Their native numbers are inbound-only with no SMS and no porting. A LiveKit win is not a Twilio rip-out; it’s a re-scoping of Twilio to transport.

“Let’s be precise: LiveKit doesn’t connect to the phone network or send a single text. The question isn’t Twilio or LiveKit — it’s how much you want to build above the media layer.”
Play 2 · Count the boxes

The conversation layer becomes their backlog

Threading, memory, session-end detection, post-call classification: four managed services that become five engineering projects (SMS session-end is a policy Orchestrator ran silently). Then they own the quality of each, forever.

“Here are the two diagrams. Every yellow box marked NEW is code your team writes, tests, operates, and pages on. What’s your roadmap cost for a quarter of conversation infrastructure?”
Play 3 · Concede the media layer

Don’t bluff on audio — pivot on it

Their turn detection, latency tooling, and audio control are genuinely better; OpenAI’s voice mode runs on their SFU. Arguing this loses the room. The pivot: that power matters when voice-pipeline control is the product. For a support/booking agent, the customer experience differentiator is remembering the caller and texting them back — the layer LiveKit doesn’t have.

“If your core problem is milliseconds and model mixing, they’re a credible choice. Is that your problem — or is it resolution rate and repeat contacts?”
Play 4 · The omnichannel wedge

Demo the thing they cannot do

This repo is the wedge, live: call, hang up unresolved, get a personalized text on the same number, reply, and the agent remembers everything — one profile across channels. On LiveKit, half that flow doesn’t exist and the other half is DIY.

“Call the demo number, then just wait. The follow-up text you receive is the product gap in one SMS.”
Play 5 · Total cost of operating

Platform fees are noise; ops and models are the bill

Their per-minute platform cost (~$0.014) undercuts nothing meaningful: ~90% of a voice minute is model vendors either way, and TTS choice dominates. What changes is headcount-shaped: a new always-on runtime, 2–3 more vendor relationships, and an audio competency. And HIPAA needs their $500/mo tier.

“You’d be trading a per-minute line item you can read on one invoice for an engineering budget you can’t.”
Field note · Where we’re exposed

Know our own soft spots before they raise them

Be ready for: ConversationRelay’s fixed pipeline (no mid-call model swap, no raw audio), no browser-voice story in this stack, TAC SDK maturity (1.0.x, rough edges this repo hit), and Intelligence v3’s PCI/HIPAA ineligibility. If the buyer leads with any of these, Play 3 honesty is the only move that keeps the room.
